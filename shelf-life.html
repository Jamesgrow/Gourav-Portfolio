Everyday weeknight cooking

"Sometimes, the hardest part of dinner isn't cooking. It's standing in front of an open fridge at 8:30 PM, completely unsure what to make."

Most recipe apps help you find recipes. Generic tools generate endless ideas. Shelf Life tries to answer a smaller, more practical question: What should I make tonight with what I already have?

The core concept: transparent ingredient match, clear time commitment, and practical kitchen directions. Masala Egg Rice · Practical Step Guidance
01 · The Problem

The Problem Isn't Finding Recipes. It's Making a Decision.

You come home tired. You open the fridge. There's half a packet of paneer, leftover dal from yesterday, some tomatoes, and milk. You have food, but you don't know what to make. You search online, get 50 complicated recipes requiring 4 spices you don't own, feel exhausted, and end up ordering takeout while fresh food sits in your fridge.

close The Typical Weeknight Loop
1 Open fridge at 8:30 PM with low energy
2 Stare at random leftover items with decision paralysis
3 Search online and get 50 complex gourmet recipes
4 Discover step 4 needs an ingredient you don't own
5 Give up, order delivery, and let the groceries expire
Result: Unnecessary expense, food waste guilt, and 45 minutes of waiting.
check The Shelf Life Direction
1 Open Shelf Life to see what's already recorded
2 Check expiring produce right at the top
3 Get 1 high-confidence Best Match + 2 rapid backups
4 See transparently why this recipe fits what's on hand
5 Cook with big timers and finish in under 20 minutes
Result: Fresh meal made on time, zero wasted groceries, and calm cooking.

02 · Why I Found This Interesting

Personal Observations & Design Hypotheses

Before sketching any wireframes, I wrote down what I noticed in my own kitchen and from conversations with friends living on their own.

Observation 01

Starting with What's Wilting

"In many Indian households, dinner doesn't start with a fancy recipe book. It starts with what needs to be eaten first — leftover dal, a couple of rotis, or vegetables that won't last until tomorrow."

Reality: Cooking is reverse-engineered from the crisper drawer.

Observation 02

Tuesday vs. Weekend Cooking

"Recipe platforms treat cooking like a weekend hobby with unlimited groceries. On a Tuesday night, cooking is about quick decisions, low cleanup, and using what's in the crisper drawer."

Reality: A 15-minute meal that dirties 3 pans is not practical.

My Design Hypothesis

The Power of Fewer Choices

"If an app limits choices to 1 strong recommendation and explains why it fits your current fridge, people might actually cook instead of feeling overwhelmed."

Hypothesis: Decisiveness beats variety when you're exhausted.

03 · Understanding the Problem

I Started by Talking to a Few Friends Who Cook for Themselves

I didn't run a corporate market research study. Instead, I had honest kitchen chats with friends who live independently and struggle with weeknight dinners.

1 Cooking starts with the crisper drawer, not a search bar

People rarely think of a dish first. They open the vegetable tray, check what's turning soft, and try to think of something they can make around it.

2 Too many options feel like homework

When you're drained after a long workday, scrolling through 30 search results doesn't feel inspiring. It feels like another chore.

3 Missing ingredients kill the momentum

Starting to cook only to find out halfway that you need a specific paste or spice causes instant frustration and ordering out.

4 Leftovers need a second life, not just reheating

Reheating cold dal in a microwave feels sad and unappetizing. But kneading yesterday's dal into crisp parathas feels clever and satisfying.

04 · Key Design Decisions

Problem → Approach → Why

I focused on making intentional decisions that reduce friction rather than piling on standard recipe app features.

Decision 01

Why I Show Fewer Recipes

The Problem: My first layout had a 10-recipe grid. It felt just as overwhelming as a search engine.

My Approach: 1 "Best Match" hero card with just 2 lightweight alternatives ("Faster" or "Lighter").

Why: Having a clear top pick gives users the confidence to stop scrolling and start cooking.
Decision 02

The "Why This Fits You" Card

The Problem: Recommendations felt mysterious and random, leading people to doubt them.

My Approach: An upfront checklist showing "You have 5 of 5 ingredients" and "Uses leftover basmati rice from yesterday."

Why: Showing the clear logic behind the suggestion builds instant trust.
Decision 03

Making Ingredient Entry Easy

The Problem: Tracking kitchen inventory can feel like doing data entry in Excel.

My Approach: Quick-tap category chips (Fridge, Pantry, Freezer) and single-tap expiry alerts ("Use Today").

Why: If logging groceries takes more than a minute, nobody will keep doing it.
Decision 04

Designing for Messy Hands in Cook Mode

The Problem: Small buttons, complex text, and tiny sliders are impossible to use while stirring a pan with oily fingers.

My Approach: Oversized 60-second timer buttons, 4-foot readable text, and a prominent voice indicator.

Why: Kitchen software has to work in actual cooking environments, not just on a sterile desk.

07 · What I Changed & What I Learned

Moments of Honest Learning

Watching a friend try to cook using my early wireframe was humbling. Here are three things I got wrong and fixed.

Iteration 01

From a 10-recipe feed to 1 Best Match + 2 alternates

My first instinct was to display a catalog of recipes so users had "freedom." In reality, they spent several minutes tapping through cards and feeling indecisive. Cutting it to a single top pick brought immediate relief.

Fewer choices, faster decisions
Iteration 02

From hidden AI match scores to an open "Why This Fits You" checklist

I had an abstract "94% AI Match" badge that felt like marketing fluff. Users asked what 94% even meant. Replacing it with a simple checklist ("Uses 5/5 ingredients at home") made the app feel honest.

Clarity over mystery badges
Iteration 03

From clinical calorie counts to practical kitchen signals

Early mockups had bold calorie badges. But when someone is tired after work, seeing calorie counts induced guilt. I replaced them with practical signals: "18 mins", "1 pan cleanup", and "Comfort level: high".

Practical everyday context
Junior Designer Reflections

Knowing what to remove

"Adding features is easy. Knowing what to remove is much harder. I spent too much time on colors before fixing the fundamental flow."

Designing for real life

"A kitchen app isn't used like a banking app. It has to survive messy hands, steam, and distracted attention on a counter."

Designing with cultural empathy

"Respecting how everyday Indian kitchens actually repurpose food without relying on decorative tropes made the project feel real."

08 · Next Horizons

What I'd Explore Next

If I continued developing this concept into a live product, here are the three areas I would experiment with next:

Exploration 01

Hands-free audio cues

Simple tone alerts when a 60-second sauté timer finishes, so you don't even have to look at the screen.

Exploration 02

Smarter receipt parsing

Allowing users to upload grocery delivery receipts to log staples without manual tapping.

Exploration 03

Regional home staples

Expanding leftover rescue ideas to include regional dishes like curd rice temperings, rasam broths, and poha snacks.

"Good design doesn't always give people more choices. Sometimes, it simply helps them choose."
